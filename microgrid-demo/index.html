How to read this demonstration

  • The model is simplified. It is a mixed-integer linear program over 48 hours, so effects such as hydraulic head in PHES and the thermodynamics of CAES are not modelled in detail. Results illustrate behaviour and are not design figures.
  • The optimiser knows the future. It sees the whole 48 hours of load, solar and price in advance, so its savings are a best case.
  • Equal nameplate does not mean equal usable capacity. Part of the difference between technologies comes from usable capacity rather than from the storage physics.
  • The load is measured campus data, scaled. Both saved periods keep their measured hourly shape and are multiplied by one factor, so that the highest hour in the saved data equals the maximum demand you set.
  • The saved periods are examples. The sunny period was chosen for its large daily price spread, so its savings are near the top of what this data can show.
  • Running costs only. Costs include grid energy, solar energy and storage operation and maintenance. The cost of building the storage is not included.
  • Efficiency curves. BESS and PHES use constant efficiency. CAES combines published machine curves with 94% motor and generator efficiency, which gives about 65% round trip at full load and is optimistic. The FCES fuel cell curve excludes balance-of-plant losses and is also optimistic.
  • Rule-based operation sends solar to the load first, then uses storage, then the grid. Surplus solar charges the storage and the rest is exported. Both rule-based and optimised operation start from the same storage level and are free to end at any level.
  • Disconnecting an element removes it for the whole two days. With the grid disconnected, the microgrid runs alone: load that solar and storage cannot meet goes unserved, and surplus solar that storage cannot take is spilled.
